Ideal form

Every range below is what a rep is graded against. It starts in youth mode, because the published ranges come from studies of proficient adults and a growing athlete graded against those fails on nearly every rep. Each row names where its range came from; the ones marked coaching cue have no published range at all. Change any of them and the row says coach set, because it is then your number and not the paper's.

Set it from a photo

A picture of the form you want. The pose model reads it in three dimensions, so the elbow, knee and wrist come out right even from a three-quarter or a rear view. Lean and release height are read against the picture, so those two need a level camera and are marked when they are not trustworthy.

    Set up the camera

    1. Sideline, square. Stand on the sideline directly across from the shooter, so the line from shooter to rim runs straight across the picture. Angled views bend the arc and hide the elbow.
    2. Shooting hand nearest the phone. A right-hander shoots with the phone on their right. The arm, wrist and ball then sit in front of the body instead of behind it.
    3. Chest height, landscape, still. A tripod or a bleacher at about 4 ft. With the 1x lens tilt the phone up until the floor runs along the bottom edge of the picture; that puts the top of the frame about 11 ft up, where the arc goes. Hold nothing; the tracker assumes the camera does not move.
    4. Fifteen feet with the 1x lens, or ten feet with the 0.5x ultra-wide held level. On a full court the sideline is about 25 feet from the free-throw line, which works with 1x but makes the ball small, so step the phone in along the square line or bring the shooter toward it. In a tight gym pick the 0.5x from the camera list and stand 10 feet off. The calculator below does the sums for the shooter's height.
    5. Tape the spot. One mark for the feet keeps every rep at the same distance, so the numbers compare across reps and days.
    6. Let it find the ball. Press "Find the ball", then bounce the ball a few times where the camera can see it. The tracker picks out the one orange thing in the gym that travels, and learns its colour under this light and its size, which is what gives every measurement its scale. If the gym defeats it, walk over and tap the ball on the picture instead.

    What it measures, and what it cannot

    From one side-view camera the tracker reads the shooting-side arm, the trunk, the legs and the head, and follows the ball for the first second of flight. Each rep is cut into dip, rise, release, follow-through and landing the way the shooting literature does: release is the frame the ball leaves the hand. Where a measured range for proficient shooters exists it is used and the paper is named beside the number. Wrist snap, follow-through hold, legs-before-arm and head aim have no published range and are marked "coaching cue". Timing is read one camera frame at a time (0.03 s at 30 fps).

    • Head aim is the ear-to-nose line, not gaze. A pose model gives eye and ear positions; it cannot see where the pupils point. The tracker reports where the head is aimed and whether it moves during the shot.
    • No elbow-under-the-ball, no side-to-side lean. Those are front-view measurements. A side view sees forward and back, up and down.
    • The arc is read until the ball leaves the top of the picture, about a third of a second with the placement above and at most a second. Enough for launch angle, release height and speed. Whether it went in is not measured; Blake can see that.
    • Orange behind the arc defeats the colour tracker. A wood floor under the ball is fine; an orange wall, banner or a second ball in the arc's path is not. Shoot toward a plain background, keep spare balls out of frame, and with the 0.5x lens keep the shooter near the centre, where the picture does not bend.
    • Open it over https. An iPhone refuses the camera on a file opened from Files. The first load fetches about 6 MB of pose model; after that it is quick.
    • A sideways tilt tilts every angle. Lean, head aim and launch angle are read against the picture's vertical, so keep the phone level side to side and still. Tilting it UP for the 1x lens is fine and is what fits the arc in; just keep the shooter near the middle of the picture. The dashed floor line shows what the tracker takes as the floor.
    • The athlete sets, then shoots. The tracker measures the dip against how the athlete stands, so it wants about a second of standing still in frame before the shot. That is how a free throw or a spot shot already looks. A shot taken on the run, a layup, or anyone whose feet are out of frame is refused rather than guessed at.
    • Nothing is recorded. The camera feed is analysed frame by frame and discarded. What stays on this phone is joint coordinates for the last few seconds and the shot cards you see, which you can clear.
